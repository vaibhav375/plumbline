Plumbline
A pre-publication checkpoint that tests copy, claims and measurable visual rules against the organisation's own rules, facts and approved wording. It shows the evidence for each issue, says what it did not check, and learns which guidelines are unclear. People make every decision.
The problemContent comes from employees, agencies, influencers and AI tools faster than a brand team can review it. Wrong claims, stale facts, off-brand tone and visuals, and accessibility gaps reach the public, and the same mistakes repeat because corrections never feed back.
Who has itSmall brand teams in mid-size organisations (first: Indian D2C personal care) who govern content from dozens of creators; compliance reviewers who sign off claims; and creators and agencies, who wait days for vague feedback.
How I solve itTurn the brand book into testable rules and a live fact ledger with owners, expiry dates and approved wording. Code checks first, AI only for judgement. Every finding cites its source, every report lists what was not checked, and claims are fixed only with approved wording. Legal and accessibility rules block from day one; brand rules earn the right to block.

Brand governance used to be a design question, such as whether the logo sat on the right background. It is now a question of volume, accuracy and legal liability.

1.3 Why now: in India, a wrong claim now costs the brand and the endorser

Misleading ads and endorsements

The CCPA's guidelines, notified on 9 June 2022 under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, set conditions for non-misleading ads and disclaimers and make endorsers do due diligence. Penalties reach ₹10 lakh, or ₹50 lakh for repeat violations, for manufacturers and endorsers. [26]

Greenwashing

The CCPA's Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Greenwashing or Misleading Environmental Claims (15 October 2024) require terms such as "eco-friendly", "natural" and "sustainable" to be qualified and substantiated. [13]

Influencer disclosure

ASCI's influencer guidelines (in force since 2021) require an upfront label from an approved list (Advertisement, Ad, Sponsored, Collaboration, Partnership, Employee, Free gift) whenever there is a material connection, superimposed on image-only posts. In FY2024-25, 98% of the 1,015 influencer ads ASCI investigated needed changes. [5][27]

Beyond India (roadmap regimes)

The EU bans generic green claims from 27 September 2026 [11]; the UK CMA can fine up to 10% of global turnover since April 2025 [12]; the European Accessibility Act applies from 28 June 2025. [14]

1.4 Root causes (my analysis)

  1. Guidelines live in PDFs, not where content is made. Agencies, influencers and AI tools never see them at the moment of writing.
  2. Facts have no single owner, expiry date or approved wording. Prices, study results and launch dates change; old copy keeps being reused, and nobody can find where an outdated claim is still live.
  3. Review is manual, late and sequential. Mechanical issues use up reviewer time that should go to judgement calls.
  4. Checks look for what is wrong, not for what is missing. Required disclaimers, risk warnings and paid-partnership labels are verified by memory.
  5. Feedback does not build up, and many guidelines cannot be tested as written. In the style guide I audited (§3.2), 15–20% of rules have no threshold or example, rising to more than half of its voice-and-tone rules.
Problem, in one sentence: organisations cannot check, at the speed content is now produced, that what they publish is accurate, on-brand and accessible, and they cannot learn from the corrections they already make.

3.2 Original observation: how testable is a real style guide?

Method. I took the Mailchimp Content Style Guide (public, CC BY-NC 4.0), a widely cited mature guide, and listed every directive rule in five sections: 114 rules. Each was given one class, and a second rater classified all 114 blind. Appendix B lists every rule and both ratings.

A · code-checkable

A pattern, word list, count or markup check decides it.
e.g. "Use the % symbol instead of spelling out percent"

B · needs understanding

Needs language or visual understanding, but has a clear criterion.
e.g. "Use positive language rather than negative language"

C · ambiguous as written

No threshold or example; careful reviewers could disagree.
e.g. "Use emoji infrequently and deliberately"

A code-checkableB needs understandingC ambiguous as written

An AI baseline, to be compared with human rating. Both raters so far are AI-assisted (the author working with Claude, and Claude run blind). I use this AI rating deliberately as a baseline: in Round 2, human raters will classify the same rules blind, and I will report how closely AI and human judgement agree. That comparison matters to the product itself, because Plumbline relies on AI to sort rules into these classes. Until then, these figures show the scheme is applied consistently by AI, not that humans agree. The blind AI rater agreed with the first rater on 94 of 114 rules (82.5%, Cohen's κ = 0.72). It found more ambiguity, not less: 23 rules (20%) ambiguous overall and 9 of 11 voice-and-tone rules; only one rule left class C (the contrast rule, which WCAG makes measurable). Notable details: five separate rules say "sparingly" or "rarely" (emoji, exclamation points, ellipses, semicolons, hashtags) without a number, and the accessibility section says "aim for high contrast" without the WCAG 4.5:1 ratio.

The proposed solution

4Plumbline: the solution

Value proposition. Plumbline lets any creator (an employee, an agency, an influencer or an AI tool) publish content the brand team would approve. It checks content against the organisation's own rules, facts and approved wording, shows the evidence for every issue, says what it did not check, and fixes problems without inventing claims or losing what the creator meant.

4.1 Six principles that balance automation with human control

1 · Code first, AI second

Plain code decides whatever code can decide (nearly half the rules in the guide I audited). A code-only mode makes no external calls.

2 · Evidence or silence; silence is not clearance

A finding without a valid citation and quote is never shown, and every report lists what was not checked. No pass badge or score.

3 · Suggest, never silently change

One-click fixes still need a click. Nothing is auto-published, and accessibility and claim fixes are never applied automatically.

4 · Never invent a claim

Regulated claims are fixed only with approved wording from the ledger. Other rewrites must pass five intent checks.

5 · Two tiers of rules

Legal and accessibility rules block from go-live, after a setup back-test. Brand rules start advisory and earn the right to block once reviewers reliably agree with them.

6 · Confidential facts stay out of prompts

Confidential facts never enter a prompt. Draft text that matches them is masked, with the catch rate measured; code-only mode closes the gap. Agencies never see a match.

4.6 Insights, guideline health and claim recall

Insights (team leads)

  • Recurring issues by rule, channel and origin (employee, agency, AI tool), from submitted content only
  • First-pass clean rate, review rounds, time to approval
  • Claim coverage, and facts expiring in the next 30 days
  • Claim recall: when a fact changes or expires, every published or scheduled asset using it gets an update-or-withdraw task for its owner

Guideline health (brand owner)

  • Override rate and reviewer disagreement per rule (v1 at Round 2)
  • Rule maturity: brand rules graduate to blocking when the 95% lower bound of reviewer agreement reaches 80%. In Round 2 the decisions come from testers labelling a back-test of 60 past posts, so only frequent rules will graduate that early.
  • Dormant and conflicting rules; reviewer feedback that contradicts the rulebook ("moving goalposts")
  • A drafted clearer rule from real accepted and dismissed cases, for the owner to approve
Patterns, not people. Pre-checks never feed insights. No breakdown is shown for groups smaller than three people, there are no individual leaderboards, and an agency sees the same views about its own work that the client sees, including how often the client's reviewers contradicted their own rulebook.

Responsible design

7Privacy, security and human oversight

Privacy and confidentiality

  • Every fact and asset carries a label: Public, Internal, Confidential or Embargoed-until-date. Confidential and embargoed facts are checked by code only and never placed in a prompt; draft spans that match them are masked before any AI call, and I measure how often the matcher catches paraphrases. Paraphrases it misses could reach the AI, so sensitive launches can run in code-only mode.
  • Code-only mode: organisations that cannot send content out run only the rule engine (nearly half the rules in the guide I audited) with zero external calls.
  • PII is redacted before any AI call; only the needed spans and rules are sent; embeddings are computed locally.
  • An exportable egress ledger records which text went to which model and region, and when.
  • Designed for India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023: personal data is minimised and redacted, processed under a data processing agreement, and deleted on request.
  • I use Anthropic's commercial API, whose terms exclude training on API inputs by default (re-confirmed at build time); data residency options are reviewed per customer.

Security

  • Row-level security on every table, with automated cross-tenant access tests. For each job the app issues the worker a short-lived token scoped to that job's organisation, so row-level security still applies; the worker holds no service key.
  • The audit log is insert-only (UPDATE and DELETE revoked) and hash-chained; the daily chain head is e-mailed (via a disclosed SMTP provider) to the brand owner and compliance as an external anchor, so later edits are detectable. It exports to CSV.
  • Private storage with short-lived signed links; file type and size checks; uploaded files are parsed in a sandboxed process with no database credentials; secrets outside the repository.
  • Prompt injection: content is delimited data; the model has no tool that can change state; outputs are schema-validated and citation-checked; I test both "approve me" and "hide this issue" attacks; only people approve. Cache keys include rulebook, model and prompt versions.

Human oversight

  • Autonomy dial per category: one-click fix (mechanical only, still needs a click), suggest, or require a person. Claims and accessibility can never be set to one-click.
  • Mandatory legal and accessibility rules block from day one; brand rules advise until they earn the right to block.
  • Creators cannot dismiss blocking findings; compliance decides claim findings with a second approver; each function owns its own rules.
  • Nothing publishes without a human approval bound to a version hash.

Transparency and explainability

  • Each finding shows its rule or fact ID, the source excerpt, the reason and how it was decided (rule engine, or AI with model name and support-check result). A confidence number appears only after calibration on held-out data.
  • Every report carries a coverage statement and never a pass badge, score or "compliant" label.
  • A "What leaves this system" page lists every external service, what data it receives and why.
  • Results are traceable: each run stores its rulebook, model and prompt versions, inputs and outputs.

Fairness, accessibility and people

  • Accessibility is a floor: the brand owner cannot weaken it, contrast wins over palette, and only an accessibility approver can decide those findings.
  • Plumbline never writes alt text or claims; it asks the creator.
  • Tone rules must not penalise regional varieties of English; the evaluation set includes them.
  • "Patterns, not people": private pre-checks, no breakdown for groups under three, symmetric views for agencies. The interface targets WCAG 2.2 AA.

Limits I state up front

  • Automated accessibility checks cover only part of WCAG; reports say so.
  • AI judgement can be wrong. Citations, support checks and confidence make errors visible, and people decide.
  • Not legal advice and not a regulatory sign-off; legal approval stays with qualified reviewers.
  • Photography mood, implied claims in imagery and video are out of scope until proven measurable.

  1. Your role, and the size of your organisation
  2. How many people or agencies create content your team reviews?
